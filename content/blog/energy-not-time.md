Time management treats every hour as interchangeable. Anyone who has tried to
write something difficult at 4pm after four meetings knows that they are not.

You have perhaps three or four hours a day of genuinely sharp attention. The
rest is useful for different kinds of work, and the skill is matching the work
to the hour rather than trying to force the sharp work into whatever gap
appears.

## Find your shape

For a week, note two things at the end of each day: when the work felt easy,
and when it felt like pushing a car. You are looking for a shape, not a
schedule — most people find one strong morning stretch, a genuine dip after
lunch, and a smaller second wind.

Then stop fighting the dip. The dip is a fine place for expenses, errands,
one-to-ones, and the kind of reading that does not require you to hold five
things in your head.

## The categories are not "hard" and "easy"

A more useful split is by what the work demands:

- **Generative** — writing, designing, debugging something genuinely unknown.
  Needs your best hours, uninterrupted.
- **Evaluative** — reviewing, deciding between known options, giving feedback.
  Survives moderate energy, and benefits from a little distance.
- **Mechanical** — filing, formatting, scheduling, the long tail of admin.
  Works fine when you are tired, and is weirdly satisfying then.

Mechanical work in your best hour is the most expensive mistake on this list,
and it is the easy one to make, because mechanical work feels productive
immediately.

## Protecting the input side

Energy is mostly determined before the day starts: sleep, what the evening
looked like, whether you ate. None of that is novel advice and all of it
outperforms any scheduling trick.

The scheduling trick only decides how well you *spend* the budget. It does not
set the budget.
