Calendars are built for meetings, so they fill up with meetings. Nothing on a
calendar says "this is the part of the day where the real work happens", which
means the real work gets whatever is left over — usually forty-five minutes
after lunch, sliced in half by a stand-up.

The fix is unglamorous: book the block like it is a meeting, because to
everyone else's scheduling software, it is.

## Make it two hours, not four

Four-hour blocks look impressive and get cancelled. Two hours survives contact
with a real week, and it is long enough to clear the twenty-minute ramp-up
before you are actually thinking.

If two hours is impossible, take ninety minutes. Below an hour you are not
making anything; you are just remembering where you left off.

## Protect the start, not the end

The block dies at the front. One "quick question" at minute three costs you the
whole ramp-up, while an interruption at minute ninety costs you almost nothing.

So the rules are front-loaded:

- Chat and mail closed for the first forty-five minutes. Not minimised — closed.
- Phone in another room, face down is not far enough.
- A note on the block saying what you are making, so future-you does not spend
  the first ten minutes choosing.

## Decide the night before

The single highest-leverage habit here is writing down the block's one
deliverable at the end of the previous day, while the context is still warm.
A block that starts with "what should I work on?" is already half gone.

> If you cannot name the output in one sentence, the block is not ready — it
> needs a planning pass first, and that is a different kind of work.

## What it is not for

Not email. Not code review. Not "catching up". Those are real work, but they
are the work that survives being chopped up, which is exactly why they belong
in the fragmented parts of the day.
