There is a particular kind of busy that feels like progress: migrating notes to
a new app, rebuilding a task system, finding a better calendar. It is tidying
dressed as work, and it is seductive precisely because it is genuinely
pleasant.

A tool earns its place by removing a decision, not by adding a feature.

## The ongoing tax

Each tool costs more than its learning curve. It costs the choice of where
something goes, the sync that breaks, the second place to look when you cannot
find a note, and the eventual migration when it shuts down.

Two or three well-worn tools beat seven good ones, mostly because you stop
spending attention on *where things live*.

## Defaults do more than features

Most of the benefit of any setup comes from a handful of defaults:

- Where a new note lands, without you choosing.
- What opens when you start the day.
- Which list is the one you actually look at.

Spend the effort there. A plain text file with a good default location
outperforms a sophisticated system you have to think about.

## A reasonable test before adopting anything

Before adding a tool, answer three questions honestly:

1. What decision does this remove?
2. What am I removing to make room for it?
3. If it disappeared tomorrow, how bad would it be?

Question three is the useful one. If the answer is "not very", you have found
something optional, and optional things are exactly the ones worth cutting
while the week is already full.

## Let things get old

A system you have used unchanged for two years is not stagnant — it is
load-bearing. The urge to rebuild it usually shows up when the real work is
hard, which is the moment to be most suspicious of it.
